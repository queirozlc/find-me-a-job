# How ATS and Job-Matching Systems Rank and Match Candidates

Research date: 2026-09-01. Every claim below is tied to a fetched or searched source; items without a specific reading are marked "not observable" or "search-summary only" (I did not open the primary page).

## 1. Retrieval layer: recruiter search mechanics

**Core engine.** Elasticsearch/Lucene and BM25 are the standard ranking function underlying virtually all production keyword search, including recruiting search products — this is a general IR fact, not an ATS-specific disclosure I read. [BM25 explainer, Elastic Blog](https://www.elastic.co/blog/practical-bm25-part-2-the-bm25-algorithm-and-its-variables) (undated, vendor docs). BM25 scores on term frequency, inverse document frequency, and document-length normalization — this is generic IR, confirmed generically, not confirmed specifically inside any named ATS's source.

**LinkedIn Recruiter — read directly.** LinkedIn's own engineering blog (peer-reviewed-adjacent engineering blog, read in full) describes a two-layer architecture: L1 does distributed candidate retrieval/initial ranking across index partitions on **Galene**, LinkedIn's Lucene-based search system; L2 re-ranks the shortlist with more expensive, dynamic features. Ranking evolved from linear models → Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT) → pairwise learning-to-rank GBDT (compares candidates within the same search request) → GLMix (GBDT blended with Generalized Linear Mixed models for per-recruiter/per-contract personalization). Query expansion uses unsupervised LINE (Large-Scale Information Network Embeddings) trained on co-occurrence, so a search for "Software Engineer" also surfaces "Software Developer." The success metric is not classic relevance but **InMail Accept** (candidate replies positively to a recruiter InMail), optimized via precision@k. [LinkedIn Engineering — AI Behind LinkedIn Recruiter Search](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/recommendations/ai-behind-linkedin-recruiter-search-and-recommendation-systems) (engineering blog, read in full, date not visible in fetch).

**Boolean search exposure.** Confirmed by search-summary (not read verbatim) that Greenhouse, Lever, Workday, and iCIMS support Boolean operators (AND/OR/NOT, quotes, parentheses) in candidate search, and LinkedIn Recruiter exposes **separate Boolean fields per filter category** (title, company, skills queried independently), on top of 40+ structured filters. [Greenhouse support article via search summary](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/202360199-Search-candidates-using-Boolean-queries); [Sprout Blog Boolean guide, search-summary, SEO/vendor blog](https://www.usesprout.com/blog/boolean-search-complete-guide-recruiters-researchers). These are SEO/marketing-blog-quality sources except the Greenhouse support page, which is vendor docs.

**Field boosting / recency decay / facets.** Not observable — I found no vendor page disclosing exact field-boost weights (title vs. skills vs. body) or a recency-decay formula for any named ATS. This is treated as a trade secret industry-wide; general Elasticsearch capability (boost per field, function_score decay) is documented by Elastic generically but I have no evidence any specific ATS publishes its actual weights.

**Shift away from Boolean.** hireEZ and others are reported (search-summary, vendor-comparison blog, not primary) to be moving toward natural-language query parsing instead of Boolean strings. [Recruiterflow/Findem comparison blogs, SEO-blog tier](https://www.findem.ai/blog/seekout-vs-hireez-vs-eightfold-vs-beamery-vs-findem).

## 2. Semantic / ML layer

**LinkedIn.** Beyond the L1/L2 architecture above, LinkedIn has published academic work: "Towards Deep and Representation Learning for Talent Search at LinkedIn" (arXiv 1809.06473, peer-reviewed-adjacent, found via search, not fetched in full) and "Learning to Retrieve for Job Matching" (arXiv 2402.13435, found via search, not fetched). A two-tower model trained with softmax loss minimizes cosine distance between a "request" (searcher/job-seeker) embedding tower and a "job" embedding tower; text (query, profile, job title/description) is encoded and concatenated with entity features. Serving uses **Zelda** (LinkedIn's retrieval infra) for approximate nearest-neighbor search over indexed job embeddings at request time. LinkedIn's newer **JUDE** (Job Understanding Data Expert) system fine-tunes LLMs to generate job embeddings, reported (vendor/LLMOps database entry, not primary paper) to yield +2.07% qualified applications, -5.13% dismiss-to-apply ratio, +1.91% total applications. [ZenML LLMOps DB entry on JUDE](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/jude-large-scale-llm-based-embedding-generation-for-job-recommendations) (third-party database summarizing a LinkedIn case study — secondhand, not the primary LinkedIn source).

**Indeed.** Indeed Engineering Blog (search-summary only, not fetched) describes a "UBM" (User Behavior Modeling) system that distills long-tail user interaction histories into compact embeddings consumable by latency-sensitive online ranking models, addressing the tension between rich deep-learning features and millisecond-scale serving in job search. [Indeed Engineering Blog, June 2026 post](https://engineering.indeedblog.com/blog/2026/06/distilling-long-tail-user-behavior-into-scalable-embeddings-for-job-search/) (engineering blog).

**Eightfold AI.** Vendor claims (search-summary, vendor site/press, not independently verified) of neural-network matching trained on "1 billion+ profiles," "1 million+ unique skills," 50+ patents, 35+ peer-reviewed publications, and an "Equal Opportunity Algorithm" for bias mitigation. [Eightfold Talent Intelligence Platform page](https://eightfold.ai/learn/talent-intelligence-platform/) and [Eightfold engineering blog on fair matching](https://eightfold.ai/engineering-blog/ai-powered-talent-matching-the-tech-behind-smarter-and-fairer-hiring/) — both vendor marketing/engineering-blog tier; treat scale figures as unverified vendor claims, not measured facts.

**ZipRecruiter.** Vendor claims (search-summary) of an AI matching layer that cut mismatched applications by 47% and raised well-matched applications by 26% in-app. [ZipRecruiter blog](https://www.ziprecruiter.com/blog/ziprecruiter-tells-you-how-strong-a-match-you-are-for-every-job/) — vendor blog, self-reported metrics, no independent audit found.

**hireEZ, Beamery, Phenom, SeekOut.** Only search-summary evidence, no primary docs fetched. hireEZ: ML-based profile standardization/enrichment across sourced data plus a move toward natural-language query parsing. Beamery: "TalentGraph," a unified candidate/employee/company profile layer underpinning relationship-based (not pure ad-hoc search) sourcing. SeekOut: "SeekOut Assist" converts natural language to structured search criteria; ships 300+ "Power Filters." [Findem comparison blog](https://www.findem.ai/blog/seekout-vs-hireez-vs-eightfold-vs-beamery-vs-findem) — vendor-comparison/SEO-blog tier, treat cautiously.

**Naukri, Seek.** Not observable — no results returned specific engineering disclosures for these two vendors in this pass.

## 3. Skill taxonomies and ontologies

**LinkedIn Skills Graph — read directly.** LinkedIn's taxonomy (as of the fetched blog post) has **~39,000 skill concepts, 374,000 aliases across 26 locales, 200,000+ edges** between skills, having grown ~35% since February 2021. Each canonical "skill concept" carries an alias list (abbreviations like "ML," translations, spelling variants) — the blog states this explicitly as the mechanism that collapses variant strings to one node, which is direct evidence for the "Node.js vs NodeJS vs Node" question, though the blog's own examples are different terms ("Cadence" → "Cadence Software," "Boundary" → "Boundary Line"), not the Node.js case itself. Construction is hybrid: human taxonomists resolve ambiguous terms using real usage metadata and manually assign parent/child relationships; a model called **KGBert** (inspired by academic KG-BERT) predicts parent-child skill edges and reportedly beats prior models by >20% F1, trained on and validated against the human-curated data. [LinkedIn Engineering — Building and maintaining the skills taxonomy](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/data/building-maintaining-the-skills-taxonomy-that-powers-linkedins-skills-graph) (engineering blog, fetched and read).

**LinkedIn Skills Genome.** A separate, earlier concept: the "skills genome" of a job/occupation is the set of skills most unique to and representative of that job, derived from skills members feature on profiles who hold that title — used to characterize emerging job categories that lack an established taxonomy entry. [LinkedIn Engineering, 2019](https://engineering.linkedin.com/blog/2019/how-we-mapped-the-skills-genome-of-emerging-jobs) (engineering blog, search-summary only, not fetched in full).

**ESCO.** European Commission's multilingual (EU languages) Skills/Competences/Qualifications/Occupations classification, cross-walked to ISCO occupation codes; ~13,890 distinct skill concepts at its most granular (Level-4) tier. [search-summary of ESCO-related academic sources](https://www.escoe.ac.uk/projects/making-sense-of-skills/) — peer-reviewed-adjacent/government source, not fetched directly.

**Resume-to-taxonomy mapping mechanism (general pattern, not vendor-specific).** Search-summarized academic literature describes matching extracted skill mentions to the taxonomy node with the closest embedding similarity (example given: "Excel" → "use spreadsheets software"), i.e., synonym/alias resolution plus semantic nearest-neighbor lookup, not exact string match. One case-study figure cited (unverified, single source, likely a vendor/case-study claim I did not verify): "a global consulting firm mapped 85% of extracted skill mentions to canonical IDs, improving job-candidate similarity scores by 23%" — I could not identify or verify the primary source of this figure; treat as unverified.

**O*NET.** U.S. Department of Labor database; occupational analysts and incumbents rate each of 35 specific skills and 41 work activities per occupation on a 1–5 importance scale (and separately a level scale), collected in rotating cycles of ~100 occupations. [O*NET Center analyst procedures PDF](https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/AOSkills_Proc.pdf) (government primary source, search-summary only, not fetched in full).

**Textkernel.** Read directly (Tx Platform developer docs, vendor API documentation). Textkernel's **Skills API** exposes Extract, Normalize, Autocomplete, and Lookup Skills endpoints; a separate **Professions API** does the same for job titles; the **Ontology API** sits on top and exposes skill↔skill and skill↔profession relations (e.g., "Suggest Skills From Professions," "Skills Similarity Score") drawn from Textkernel's proprietary knowledge graph, curated by "data mining... quality assured by domain experts." Supports 10 languages. The docs page I fetched did **not** disclose taxonomy size (node/edge counts) — not observable from this source. [Textkernel Tx Platform — Ontology API Overview](https://developer.textkernel.com/tx-platform/v10/skills-intelligence/ontology-api/overview/) (vendor docs, fetched and read).

**Lightcast/EMSI Open Skills, Textkernel "Knowledge Graph" as product name.** Not independently verified in this pass beyond the general Textkernel Ontology docs above — did not fetch Lightcast primary documentation. Not observable in detail.

## 4. Title normalization and seniority inference

General pattern (search-summarized, multiple SEO/data-vendor blogs plus one peer-reviewed source, not a single authoritative primary source): title normalization is typically a two-step process — (1) strip/extract seniority modifiers ("Sr.", "Lead", "Jr.", "VP", "Chief") into a separate seniority field via dictionary/rule matching, then (2) map the remaining role phrase to a canonical functional-taxonomy title via embedding similarity or a trained classifier, so "Sr. Software Engineer" → role: "Software Engineer" + seniority: "Senior." Most taxonomies cited use four seniority tiers: entry, mid/associate, senior, lead/manager. [JobsPikr blog, SEO-tier](https://www.jobspikr.com/blog/normalising-data-job-titles-skills-locations/); [Perfectly Hired blog, SEO-tier](https://perfectlyhired.com/knowledge-hub/normalize-job-titles).

**Academic model (JAMES).** A published model (arXiv 2202.10739, found via search, not fetched) uses multi-aspect graph embeddings plus HDBSCAN clustering for job-title normalization, reporting a 10.06% Precision@10 improvement over baseline — this is an academic benchmark result, not evidence of production deployment at any named vendor.

**Textkernel.** A Textkernel newsroom post (search-summary only) references "Deep Learning for Categorizing Job Titles" as a product capability; no methodology detail was read. [Textkernel newsroom](https://www.textkernel.com/newsroom/deep-learning-for-categorizing-job-titles/) — vendor PR, not fetched in full.

**Effect on matching.** Not independently quantified in any source read here beyond the general claim (unverified, from the earlier consulting-firm figure) that better skill/title canonicalization improved similarity-score quality by 23% in one unverified case.

## 5. Knockout questions and rules-based gating

Search-summary evidence only (SEO/HR-blog tier — Hirevire, SkillFuel, JobWizard, 100hires, Truffle — none read in full, none authoritative/primary). Consistent pattern across sources: knockout/qualifying questions are a **hard pre-filter that runs before any ranking or ML scoring** — a mandatory application-form question with exactly one acceptable answer (e.g., "Are you authorized to work in the US?", "Are you 18+?", minimum years of experience, shift availability). A "no" or disqualifying answer typically auto-rejects or auto-tags the candidate before a recruiter or scoring model ever sees them, i.e., it is a Boolean gate applied at ingestion, separate from and prior to the ranked-retrieval layer described in §1. Platforms cited as supporting this natively: Workable, Greenhouse, Lever, Breezy HR, Ashby, SmartRecruiters (search-summary, not verified per-vendor). One critique cited (Harvard Business School research, referenced secondhand via an SEO blog, not the primary HBS study itself) claims rigid exact-year-of-experience knockouts can screen out up to 63% of otherwise-qualified candidates — this figure is cited secondhand and I did not locate/read the underlying HBS study, so treat as unverified pending primary-source confirmation.

**Required vs. preferred.** Not observable in detail — no source read explained the specific mechanism (e.g., score penalty vs. hard filter) ATS products use to differentiate "required" from "preferred" qualifications at the ranking-model level, as opposed to the knockout-question layer.

## 6. Regulatory constraints and published disclosures

### NYC Local Law 144

Requires employers using an "Automated Employment Decision Tool" (AEDT) on NYC-resident candidates to: (1) commission an independent annual bias audit, (2) publicly post a summary, (3) give candidates 10 business days' notice before use. Effective January 1, 2023; enforced by NYC DCWP since July 5, 2023; penalties $500 (first violation) up to $1,500/day ongoing. [Multiple compliance blogs, search-summary, SEO/consulting-blog tier](https://verifywise.ai/blog/nyc-local-law-144-compliance-checklist-for-employers).

**Primary evidence — HireVue bias audit PDF, read directly.** I fetched and read (via PDF page extraction) the actual "New York City Local Law 144 'Bias Audit' for HireVue," conducted by **DCI Consulting Group**, summary produced July 5, 2023, covering applicants assessed January 1, 2021 – December 31, 2022. Key disclosed mechanics:
- HireVue's AEDT assigns each applicant to one of **three tiers: Bottom, Middle, Top** (the report does not disclose the underlying feature weights that produce the tier, only the tiering output).
- Impact ratio = focal-group selection rate ÷ comparator-group (highest-selection-rate) selection rate; NYC's four-fifths-rule threshold is 0.80.
- Actual figures read directly from the "Adaptability" competency table (Intern/New College Graduate jobs): Male selection rate 0.68 (comparator), Female 0.65, impact ratio 0.95. By race: Hispanic/Latino 0.69 (comparator), White 0.68 (ratio 0.98), Black/African American 0.67 (0.96), Asian 0.63 (0.92), Two or More Races 0.67 (0.94). No Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander or Native American/Alaska Native applicants had sufficient sample. Intersectional table shows e.g. Female Two or More Races at ratio 0.79 — below the 0.80 four-fifths threshold, the one subgroup in this table under the line. A separate "Communication" competency table for the same job category shows Male 0.66 (comparator) vs Female 0.65 (0.98), and race comparator White 0.70 vs Asian 0.62 (0.87).
- Data are audited **per AEDT implementation per employer**, then statistically aggregated across employers using log-averaging for impact ratios (explicitly disclosed methodology) — the report explicitly notes this NYC-mandated aggregation method is not aligned with standard adverse-impact practice (citing Morris & Dunleavy 2017) but was followed because the law requires it.
[HireVue NYC LL144 Bias Audit Report, hosted by Pfizer](https://cdn.pfizer.com/pfizercom/CareersEmploymentDocs/HireVue_2023_Bias_Report_14AUG2023.pdf) — regulatory filing / third-party audit report, **read directly, primary source**.

**Workday/HiredScore, search-summary only, not fetched.** A TIAA-commissioned LL144 audit of Workday's HiredScore "Spotlight" candidate-matching tool, independently audited by DLA Piper, reportedly found no impact ratios below the 0.80 threshold prior to a January 28, 2026 go-live. I did not fetch this PDF directly to verify; treat as search-summary only. [TIAA Workday LL144 audit PDF, cloudfront-hosted](https://dokumfe7mps0i.cloudfront.net/oms/16274/document/2026/1/doclib_455682_EHALD_1767905696374054325_1767905696432/1767905696432.pdf).

**HireVue's own disclosure of audit practice.** HireVue press release states it engaged DCI Consulting Group as external auditor for its algorithms in January 2023, covering "competency-based and game-based algorithms," across "multiple job levels and use cases." [HireVue press release](https://www.hirevue.com/press-release/hirevue-leads-industry-in-fair-and-ethical-hiring-practice-engaging-external-auditor-dci-consulting-group-for-external-bias-audit-of-algorithms) — vendor press release, search-summary only.

### EU AI Act

Annex III, Point 4 classifies AI systems used for recruitment/selection as high-risk when used to place targeted job ads, filter applications, or evaluate candidates — explicitly including CV filtering and candidate evaluation. Also covers post-hire uses (promotion, termination, task allocation, performance monitoring). Main enforcement date for these obligations: **December 2, 2027**. Non-compliance fines up to €15M or 3% of global turnover, whichever is higher. [artificialintelligenceact.eu Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/) — this is a reference/tracking site for the regulation, not the Official Journal text itself; treat as a reliable secondary/tertiary source, search-summary only, not fetched directly.

### Illinois AI Video Interview Act (AIVIA / 820 ILCS 42)

Requires employers using AI to analyze recorded video interviews to: notify applicants before the interview that AI may be used, explain (before the interview) how the AI works and what general characteristics it evaluates, and obtain consent — notice required at least 5 business days ahead. Prohibits sharing videos beyond those with a legitimate evaluative need; applicants can request deletion within 30 days. Full enforcement reported as beginning February 2026. [Justia statute text / Introl and Seyfarth law-firm summaries](https://law.justia.com/codes/illinois/chapter-820/act-820-ilcs-42/) — statute is primary law; the summaries are law-firm blogs, search-summary only, not fetched directly.

### Mobley v. Workday (litigation, not a regulator)

Filed Feb. 21, 2023; plaintiff alleges Workday's algorithmic screening (including the "Candidate Skills Match" feature and later HiredScore AI features after acquisition) disproportionately rejected applicants who are Black, disabled, and/or over 40 — plaintiff reportedly submitted 100+ applications through Workday-powered ATSes and was rejected every time. Case proceeded as a collective action; as of the search results, a court denied a motion to compel Workday's internal bias-testing data on attorney-client-privilege grounds, meaning the actual scoring logic remains **not disclosed** through this litigation as far as I found. [Duane Morris blog on the ruling](https://blogs.duanemorris.com/classactiondefense/2026/06/02/california-federal-court-clarifies-limits-on-ai-bias-testing-and-applicant-data-disclosure-in-mobley-v-workday/) — law-firm blog, search-summary only, not fetched directly. This case is significant precisely because it demonstrates the limits of what litigation currently forces vendors to disclose about scoring internals — the privileged bias-testing data was not produced.

## 7. Score composition (weights: skills vs. titles vs. education vs. tenure)

**Not observable as a general, cross-vendor fact.** No source in this research disclosed a specific numeric weighting scheme (e.g., "skills = 40%, title match = 30%, education = 15%, tenure = 15%") for any named ATS or matching vendor. The HireVue audit report — the single most detailed primary regulatory disclosure obtained — discloses the **output** of its algorithm (tier assignment: Bottom/Middle/Top, and per-competency labels like "Adaptability" and "Communication") but explicitly does **not** disclose the underlying feature weights or model architecture that produce those tiers; this appears to be the norm — bias audits under LL144 are required to test disparate-impact outcomes, not to require disclosure of model internals or feature weights. LinkedIn's disclosed features (title match, skill match — both exact and semantic —, company, location, response-likelihood signals, combined via GBDT/GLMix) is the most granular *feature list* found, but LinkedIn did not disclose relative feature-importance weights in the fetched blog post. Eightfold and other vendors describe qualitative concepts ("holistic" matching, "adjacent skills," "potential" over "keyword overlap") without quantified weights. **Conclusion: published score-composition weighting is not available from any source found in this research; this is consistent with vendors treating exact scoring weights as trade secrets even where they are compelled to disclose audit outcomes.**
