# Resume Spec: Evidence Base and Machine/Human-Readable Format

## 1. Recruiter reading behavior: what is actually measured

**The Ladders studies (industry, not peer-reviewed).** The Ladders ran two eye-tracking studies: 2012 (n and device undisclosed in any source I could access) reporting **6 seconds** average scan time, and 2018 reporting **7.4 seconds**, described as a "two-stage" study (a speed-focused stage, then an eye-tracking stage) ([HR Dive, 2018-11-08](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/); [Ladders 2018 PDF](https://www.theladders.com/static/images/basicSite/pdfs/TheLadders-EyeTracking-StudyC2.pdf), fetch blocked with HTTP 403, so I could not verify its content directly — I only have secondary reporting on it). Both reports say resumes with clear section headers, bold titles, bulleted accomplishments, and single-column layout drew more organized (E/F-pattern) gaze paths; cluttered, multi-column, low-white-space resumes did not.

**Methodology limits (documented critique).** ERE.net's analysis found the 2018 report **does not disclose** sample size, recruiter experience level, position types, or resume lengths used, and it has **never been published in a peer-reviewed venue**, making replication impossible. The critique also notes most outlets citing "6 seconds" link to nothing, i.e., the number propagated without verification ([ERE.net, "Is the 6-Second Resume Scan a Myth?"](https://www.ere.net/articles/is-the-6-second-resume-scan-a-myth)). Treat both Ladders numbers as **industry marketing research, not scientific evidence** — directionally plausible, not verified figures.

**Peer-reviewed replication (partial).** One genuine peer-reviewed paper exists: Piña & Petersheim et al., "Using Machine Learning with Eye-Tracking Data to Predict if a Recruiter Will Approve a Resume," published in the MDPI journal at DOI covered by *2504-4990/5/3/38* (I could not load the full text — WebFetch returned HTTP 403 — so figures below come from search-result abstracts, not a source I read in full, and should be treated as **unverified secondhand summary**). Reported: an ML classifier trained on recruiter eye-tracking fixations predicted resume approval with **AUC 0.767**; total viewing time and time spent on the Experience and Education sections were the strongest predictors; this study focused specifically on computer-science resumes. This is closer to real evidence than the Ladders reports but I have not read the primary text, so I cannot confirm sample size or exact device.

**Bottom line on reading behavior:** the specific "6 seconds" / "7.4 seconds" numbers are unverifiable industry claims, not peer-reviewed facts. The directionally-supported, more-defensible claim is: recruiters scan fast, in a non-linear pattern anchored on headers and the top-left/top of each section, and structural clarity (section labels, bullets, single column) measurably helps orientation. Treat exact-second figures as folklore-adjacent, not as a design constraint to optimize to the second.

## 2. Field-experiment evidence on resume content

**Race/name discrimination (peer-reviewed, high-N, the gold standard here).** Bertrand & Mullainathan, "Are Emily and Greg More Employable than Lakisha and Jamal?", *American Economic Review* 94(4), 2004, NBER WP 9873. Design: correspondence audit — fictitious resumes sent to real help-wanted ads in Boston and Chicago newspapers, race signaled only by first name (White- vs. African-American-sounding), crossed with a high/low quality resume manipulation. Finding: White-sounding names received **50% more callbacks**; resume quality improved callbacks more for White names than for Black names. This is the canonical audit-study methodology later reused in nearly all resume field experiments ([NBER w9873](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9873)).

**Photos.** Evidence is mixed and photo-dependent, not a simple "always hurts/helps." One field-experiment line: attractive men with photos got **19.9%** callbacks vs. **9.2%** for no-photo resumes; but for women, no-photo resumes got **22% more** callbacks than resumes with attractive-women photos — i.e., photo effects appear to interact with gender and attractiveness rather than acting uniformly ([search-result summary](https://academic.oup.com/ser/article/21/3/1551/7086060); I did not read primary papers directly, so exact study identities/sample sizes are not confirmed — flag as needing primary-source verification). A separate large European field experiment (Germany/Netherlands/Spain) varied ethnicity-signaling photos and names in real vacancies to isolate appearance-based discrimination ([Oxford *Socio-Economic Review*, 2023](https://academic.oup.com/ser/article/21/3/1551/7086060)). A PNAS meta-analysis of North American field experiments (49 studies since 1989) found White applicants get **36% more callbacks** than equally qualified Black applicants and **24% more** than Latino applicants, with no significant time trend for Black applicants ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1706255114) — summary only, not fully read). **Practical implication for a US software-engineering resume: omit photo.** This is standard US practice independent of the mixed experimental evidence, and photos are actively flagged by many ATS as unparseable image content.

**Employment gaps.** A field experiment on illness-related gaps found unexplained gaps hurt callbacks, and an explained (illness) gap did better than an unexplained one: **25.6%** callback with illness-explained gap vs. **23.3%** unexplained ([ScienceDirect field experiment](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016726812030367X), summary only, primary text not read). Other audit studies find duration-of-unemployment effects are weaker than commonly assumed and vary by labor-market tightness — in high-callback-rate (tight) markets, the penalty for unemployment gaps and "interim jobs" shrinks substantially ([AEA *Papers & Proceedings*, "Determinants of Callbacks to Job Applications"](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.p20161010)). Net: gaps are a real, measured negative signal, but the effect is not fixed — a one-line factual explanation appears to help, and market conditions moderate the size of the penalty.

**Resume length (1 vs. 2 pages).** ResumeGo, "Settling the Debate," study window Oct 15–Nov 2 (year not stated in the material I retrieved), n=**482** professionals with recruiting experience, evaluating **7,712 resumes** in a forced-choice simulation, half one-page (350–500 words) and half two-page (700–850 words), matched pairs per candidate profile. Result: two-page resumes were **selected 5,375 times vs. 2,337** for matched one-page versions, with the gap larger for candidates with 10+ years of experience ([ResumeGo](https://www.resumego.net/research/one-or-two-page-resumes/) — I fetched this page directly). **Caveat, stated by ResumeGo itself:** this is a forced side-by-side simulation of paired resumes, not a live-posting callback field experiment like Bertrand & Mullainathan; recruiters knew they were comparing two resumes for the same fictional candidate, which is a different task from screening a single resume against a job requirement. Treat the 2.3x figure as suggestive, not causal, evidence from a lower-rigor design than the audit studies above. For early-to-mid-career engineers (well under 10 years), the one-page preference gap in the same data was much smaller — the report does not give an exact number for this subgroup in the material I retrieved.

**Quantified achievements.** I could not find any primary field experiment, audit study, or peer-reviewed source that measures the causal effect of numeric quantification in bullet points on callback rate. Every "40% more callbacks" / "2.5x more interviews" claim traced back through search results to SEO career-advice sites (ResumeGenius, ResumeWorded, Teal, etc.) with **no citation to an underlying study**. This is a case where a very widely repeated resume rule has **no evidence behind it** that I could verify — it is plausible on the theory that recruiters and hiring managers respond to concrete, verifiable signals (and this is consistent with general labor-economics findings that objective, checkable credentials outperform vague claims), but the specific percentage lifts in circulation are fabricated or unsourced marketing copy. Recommendation: quantify where a real, honestly-known number exists, but do not repeat the "40% more callbacks" statistic as fact.

**Tailoring / per-role customization.** Same pattern: statistics like "2x callbacks across 1.7M applications," "50% more callbacks," "83% of recruiters prefer tailored resumes" all trace to vendor blogs (Jobscan, TailorForge, Sprout, etc.) with no visible underlying dataset or methodology. One number with an actual design behind it: a field experiment on **7,287 applications** found a job-specific tailored cover letter produced a **16.4%** callback rate vs. **12.5%** for a generic cover letter vs. **10.7%** with no cover letter at all ([search-result summary](https://scale.jobs/blog/resume-changes-that-improve-callback-rates-fast) — this is itself a secondary blog citing an unnamed study; I was not able to trace it to the primary paper, so treat this figure as low-confidence, not verified). Overall: tailoring almost certainly helps (it is the mechanism by which keyword-matching and relevance work in both ATS and human screening), but I cannot verify a specific magnitude from a primary source.

## 3. Machine-friendly structure: what parsers actually need

**Section headers.** Convergent evidence across ATS-testing blogs (not academic, but consistent and mechanistically plausible: parsers do literal string/pattern matching against a known header vocabulary) — the header strings that reliably map to the correct bucket across systems are the plain, unadorned forms:
- `Experience` / `Work Experience` / `Professional Experience`
- `Education`
- `Skills` (or `Technical Skills`)
- `Summary` (or `Professional Summary`)
- `Certifications`
- `Projects`

Avoid creative or merged headers ("What I've Built," "Where I've Been") — if a parser can't classify the section, it either drops the content or dumps it into an unstructured bucket ([Jobscan ATS format guide](https://www.jobscan.co/blog/20-ats-friendly-resume-templates/) and [quickcv.io 8-system parsing test](https://quickcv.io/blog/i-tested-8-ats-systems-to-see-how-they-actually-parse-resumes) — both vendor/blog sources, not peer-reviewed, but the underlying mechanism (literal header matching) is a well-documented property of how ATS parsers work).

**Columns / layout.** Test results conflict on magnitude but agree on direction: **single column is safer.** One vendor test found single-column ~93% parse accuracy vs. two-column ~86%; another found two-column resumes drew a "critical flag" and dropped from 100 to 85 in a parsing benchmark; a third found columns parsed nearly as well (95% vs. 98%). The common technical explanation across all of them: parsers extract text in a linear stream based on PDF/DOCX internal object order, not visual position — a two-column layout risks interleaving the left and right column text mid-sentence, scrambling dates, titles, and skill lists ([vantageresume.com](https://vantageresume.com/one-column-vs-two-column-resume/), [atsverification.com](https://atsverification.com/blog/two-column-resume-ats-friendly/), [enhancv.com](https://enhancv.com/blog/are-two-column-resumes-ats-friendly/) — all vendor blogs, none peer-reviewed, but mechanistically consistent with each other). **Recommendation: single column, full width.**

**File format: DOCX vs. PDF.** Evidence is dated and shifting. Older analyses (circa 2023, cited secondhand — I could not locate or read the original Jobscan study) found DOCX outperformed PDF in parsing accuracy on 6 of 8 systems tested. More recent guidance says the gap has narrowed: **text-selectable** PDFs now parse about as cleanly as DOCX in major modern ATS (Workday, Greenhouse, Lever named specifically), but a **scanned/image-based PDF** (no embedded text layer) still fails outright ([smallpdf.com](https://smallpdf.com/blog/do-applicant-tracking-systems-prefer-resumes-in-pdf-format), [scale.jobs](https://scale.jobs/blog/pdf-vs-word-resume-format-ats-reads-correctly) — vendor blogs). **Recommendation:** submit whatever format the job posting requests; if unspecified, a text-based (not scanned/flattened) PDF is safe on modern major ATS, DOCX is the more conservative fallback if the target company's ATS is unknown or older.

**Dates, contact block, bullets.** No rigorous study specifies an optimal date format; the consistent, evidence-adjacent recommendation across ATS-format guides is **`Month YYYY – Month YYYY`** (e.g., `Jan 2023 – Present`) because ambiguous or numeric-only formats (`01/23`) are more likely to be misparsed as day/month/year depending on locale settings in the parser. Contact info should be **in the body text, not in a header/footer** — many parsers do not read header/footer regions at all, which silently drops name, phone, and email. Bullets should use a **plain, standard character** (`•` or `-`); custom bullet glyphs, Wingdings-style icons, and symbol-substitution formatting risk being read as garbage characters or ignored by text extraction.

**Encoding a tech stack.** No causal study measures this directly. The mechanistically sound and commonly recommended approach (converging across ATS-format and keyword guides): list technologies **both as a labeled, parseable inventory line** (e.g., `Skills: Ruby, Rails, PostgreSQL, AWS, React`) so exact-string keyword matching succeeds, **and** embed the specific technologies inside achievement bullets in context (e.g., "Migrated a Rails monolith's job queue from Sidekiq to..."), so a human reader sees applied, not just listed, skill. This satisfies both the literal string-match layer (ATS) and the "did they actually do the thing" judgment layer (human) — this is inference from how the two evaluation layers work, not from a study that tested it directly.

## 4. Keyword strategy: mirroring vs. stuffing

No controlled experiment I found isolates keyword density's causal effect on callback rate. What is well and consistently documented (across vendor sources, converging with how ATS technically work) is the **mechanism**: modern ATS increasingly use NLP/semantic matching rather than pure literal-string counting, meaning near-synonyms and contextual usage now count for something, but many systems still weight **exact-string matches to the job posting's specific terms** (tool names, certifications, job titles) more heavily than semantically-close paraphrases. Practical implication: mirror the **exact terms** the job description uses for hard skills/tools (if they say "Kubernetes," don't only write "container orchestration"), while writing skills into natural sentence context rather than as a bare repeated list.

**What makes stuffing visible to a human reader:** the same sources converge on this diagnostic (not experimentally tested, but consistent and plausible): keyword lists disconnected from any described action ("Skills: Java, Python, Docker, Kubernetes, Agile, Scrum, Leadership, Communication...") without evidence of use in the experience section reads as generic/copy-pasted; repeating the identical term 4+ times in slightly different phrasing in a short document reads as manipulation; buzzwords with no operational meaning ("team player," "results-driven," "detail-oriented") are explicitly called out by recruiter-perspective sources as ignored/skipped rather than credited. There is no peer-reviewed study quantifying a "stuffing threshold" — this is recruiter folk-knowledge with high face validity, not measured evidence.

## 5. Achievement-writing frameworks: primary sources

**XYZ.** Formula: *"Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y], by doing [Z]."* Primary source: **Laszlo Bock**, former SVP of People Operations at Google, popularized in his book *Work Rules!* (2015) and in public interviews/advice pieces, not in a peer-reviewed publication — it is a management-practitioner heuristic, not a tested experimental framework ([search-result summary](https://www.tealhq.com/post/xyz-resume); I did not locate or read *Work Rules!* directly, so I cannot quote Bock's original wording with certainty — treat the formula string as the widely-repeated paraphrase, not a verified verbatim quote).

**STAR.** Origin: Development Dimensions International (DDI), developed as part of their **Targeted Selection** behavioral-interviewing system, credited to Dr. William C. Byham, introduced in **1974**. STAR = Situation, Task, Action, Result. This is an interview-answer structuring framework, not originally a resume-writing framework — its use for resume bullets is a later, informal adaptation by career-advice writers ([DDI](https://www.ddi.com/solutions/behavioral-interviewing/star-method) — a vendor/company source, but DDI is the originating organization, so this is closer to a primary source than most career-advice claims).

**CAR (Challenge-Action-Result).** No verifiable originating source found. It reads as a resume-specific variant/simplification of STAR (drop "Task," keep the challenge framing), popularized by resume-writing services, not attributable to a named researcher or organization in any source I found. Treat CAR as a derivative convention, not a documented, sourced framework.

**Assessment:** none of these three frameworks has been experimentally validated (no study tests "bullets written in XYZ format get more callbacks than bullets that aren't"). They are structuring heuristics with plausible mechanisms (force concreteness, force a result, force a number) rather than evidence-backed interventions. Use them as a writing discipline, not as a claim of proven efficacy.

## 6. Software-engineering-specific evidence

I found **no controlled study** on how recruiters judge a stack migration, a change of primary language, or transferable engineering experience. What is available is converging practitioner consensus, not evidence: (a) ATS keyword-matching is real and does filter on the literal tool/language strings present in the job description, so a resume for a Python role should contain the string "Python" even if the underlying skill (e.g., general backend engineering) transfers; (b) large tech employers are commonly described as caring more about general engineering ability and depth than the specific language, but this claim comes from career-advice aggregator sites, not from any employer-side data or study; (c) the practical synthesis (inference, not evidence) is that a language/stack change should be represented **twice**: once as a plain keyword line for parseability against the target posting's vocabulary, and once as a narrative bullet showing the migration as a deliberate, results-having project (what changed, why, what the measured outcome was) — this addresses the ATS layer and the human "can this person actually do it" judgment layer simultaneously, but I have no data confirming this framing changes outcomes.

## 7. Tailoring: what to change per application

No primary source gives a clean magnitude (see §2). The highest-leverage edits, in order of mechanical importance given how ATS keyword-matching and human skimming both work (this ordering is inference from the parsing/keyword mechanics above, not from an experiment that tested edit-by-edit impact):
1. Match the job posting's exact hard-skill and tool vocabulary in the Skills line and at least once in context.
2. Reorder/promote the 2–3 most relevant bullets to the top of the most recent role.
3. Adjust the summary/headline line to mirror the target job title and top 1–2 requirements.
4. Cut or de-emphasize bullets irrelevant to the target role to keep length in check.

## Resume Spec (concrete)

**Section order:**
1. Contact block (name, phone, email, location city/state, LinkedIn/GitHub URL) — in body text, never header/footer.
2. `Summary` (optional, 2–3 lines, mirrors target job title/keywords).
3. `Skills` (flat list or short grouped lines, exact-string tool/language names).
4. `Experience` (reverse chronological).
5. `Projects` (if early-career or stack-migration story needs its own space).
6. `Education`.
7. `Certifications` (if applicable).

**Header strings to use verbatim:** `Experience`, `Education`, `Skills`, `Summary`, `Certifications`, `Projects`. Avoid stylized alternatives.

**Date format:** `Mon YYYY – Mon YYYY` (e.g., `Mar 2022 – Present`). Avoid numeric-only or day-level dates.

**Layout constraints:**
- Single column, full page width. No tables, text boxes, or multi-column skill grids.
- No photo, no graphics/icons standing in for text, no header/footer content.
- Standard bullet character (`•` or `-`), no custom glyphs.
- Standard fonts (Arial, Calibri, Georgia-class), 10.5–11.5pt body text.
- File format: text-based PDF by default; DOCX if the target ATS/company is unknown or likely older; whatever the posting explicitly requests otherwise.
- Filename convention (no direct evidence found, but consistent with parseability logic above): `FirstName-LastName-Resume.pdf` — plain text, no special characters, so downstream systems that infer applicant name from filename don't fail.

**Length:** 1 page under ~10 years experience; 2 pages acceptable and, per the ResumeGo simulation (low-to-moderate confidence, see §2), possibly preferred for 10+ years — but this data point comes from a forced-comparison simulation, not a live-posting field experiment, so treat as directional only.

**Tech stack encoding:** list in a dedicated `Skills` line for exact-match parsing, and repeat the load-bearing 2–4 technologies inside relevant experience bullets in context.

**Keyword approach:** mirror the target posting's exact hard-skill terms; avoid bare buzzwords with no supporting evidence in the experience section; do not repeat the same term more than 2–3 times across the document.

### What has real evidence vs. what is folklore (summary)

- **Real, sourced evidence:** race/name discrimination in callbacks (Bertrand & Mullainathan, peer-reviewed); employment-gap penalty and its moderation by explanation and market tightness (field experiments); parsing mechanics of single- vs. multi-column layouts (consistent mechanistic explanation across independent vendor tests, though none peer-reviewed); STAR's origin at DDI, 1974.
- **Directionally plausible but not rigorously verified:** the Ladders' 6-/7.4-second figures (unpublished methodology); ResumeGo's 1-vs-2-page result (forced-comparison simulation, not a callback field experiment); DOCX vs. PDF parsing differential (dated, shifting, vendor-sourced).
- **No credible evidence found, treat as folklore:** specific percentage lifts from quantifying achievements ("40% more callbacks"), specific percentage lifts from tailoring ("2x callbacks," "83% of recruiters"), the XYZ formula's causal effect on outcomes, any causal test of keyword-stuffing thresholds, and any study on software-engineering-specific keyword filtering or stack-migration presentation.
